For the last few years of running my company, I signed customer support emails
as Andrew.

Andrew is not my name. I picked it because customers were kinder to Andrew than
they were to Laura. Andrew got the benefit of the doubt. Andrew's answers were
accepted the first time. Nobody explained to Andrew how their own product
worked. When I eventually handed most of the first-line support to AI, I kept
the signature, partly because it was easier and partly because by then it felt
like the house style.

I want to be careful about what that story is evidence of. It is one person's
inbox. But I stopped running the experiment because I already knew the result.

## The people who bought the first boards

The first customers were builders. They bought a development board because they
wanted to make something out of it, and they arrived expecting to do work.

They read the documentation. They filed real bug reports, sometimes with a fix
attached. They understood that a small sensor company was not going to have an
answer to every question within the hour, and that some of what they were
attempting had never been attempted with our hardware before, so we would be
figuring it out together. When something worked, they told other people. When
something did not, a fair number of them told us how to fix it.

That is not nostalgia for better people. It is a description of a filter. Early
on, our product was hard enough to use that it selected for a certain kind of
customer.

## The people who bought the last boards

Then we got easier to buy from, and more people bought.

The requests changed shape. Free customization for one specific use case. Free
consulting, delivered as a support ticket. Firmware treated as free because the
SDK was open source, though the firmware never was. An answer within the hour,
on a Sunday. Amazon shipping. A refund because the sensor did not do a thing we
had never claimed it did, and a chargeback when the refund was declined.

The framing I keep coming back to is not that customers got worse. It is:

> Growth diluted the contributor class and amplified the extractor class.

The average customer did not get less intelligent. The average interaction got
less informed, less patient, and less reciprocal. What disappeared was not
competence. It was reciprocity.

## Our own Eternal September

There is a name for this from the early internet. Every September, a new
cohort of university students arrived on Usenet, behaved badly, and got
socialized by the regulars until they knew the norms. Then in late 1993
commercial providers opened the gates, the flood stopped being seasonal, and it
became known as the September that never ended.

The obvious reading is that newcomers ruin things. That is not actually what the
research says, and the more interesting version is less flattering to me.

In 2016 a group of researchers studied a Reddit community that took an enormous
newcomer surge and came out the other side intact. It survived because it had
active, coordinated moderators, enough shared identity that existing members
policed norms themselves, and technical systems that absorbed the rest. The
surge was not the deciding factor. The capacity to absorb it was.

We had that capacity once. A forum with several of us answering on it, engineers
who liked talking to customers, enough people that norms got transmitted rather
than diluted. Then the team dispersed, and it was me. One person cannot
socialize a growing customer base. One person can only answer tickets, and
eventually not even all of those.

So the newcomers did not join our culture. They became it.

## It was not only us

For a while I assumed this was a me problem, or a hardware problem.

Then Pew asked 9,609 Americans about it in late 2024 and 47% said public
behavior had got ruder since the pandemic, one in five saying a lot ruder. That
is about behavior in general rather than customers specifically, so it is
context and not proof. But it matched what the inbox felt like.

The closer research is on customer incivility itself, and it is fairly bleak.
A two-wave study of frontline service workers, surveyed before the pandemic and
again in late 2020, found the path from customer rudeness to worse job
performance, running through emotional exhaustion, became *more pronounced*
after the pandemic began. One study, one country, 211 people. Not a law of
nature. But the direction is the one everyone working a counter would guess.

At some point the bad interactions stopped feeling like exceptions and started
feeling like the operating environment.

## Open source was never a promise of free labor

The open source part deserves its own paragraph, because it is the most
misunderstood.

Publishing your SDKs does not mean you have agreed to work for free, forever,
for anyone who downloads them. Maintainers know this. Tidelift's 2024 survey of
open source maintainers found 60% are unpaid hobbyists, 48% feel underappreciated
or that the work is thankless, and 39% say users are too demanding and expect too
much of them. Sixty percent have quit a project or considered it.

One maintainer quoted in their report put it better than I can:

> Most users, even ones who require fixes, are not willing to roll up their
> sleeves to help. They just expect someone else to fix it for free.

That is the whole thing. People wanted the benefits of open source without any
of its obligations, and were often annoyed to learn there were obligations.

## The trust loop

Here is the part I find genuinely uncomfortable, because it implicates me.

Bad behavior makes a company defensive. Every abused return policy tightens the
return policy. Every chargeback adds a verification step. Every ticket demanding
an immediate answer to a question already answered in the docs pushes you toward
the canned reply, then toward the macro, then toward the bot. Warmth is
expensive, and hostility raises the price.

Your customers then experience all of that as the company getting worse. Which
it did. I did make it worse. I made it worse in direct response to what people
were doing to me, and the people who got the colder version were mostly not the
people who caused it.

We talk about enshittification as something platforms do to users, extracting
more and giving less until the thing is hollow. Cory Doctorow named that, and he
was right about it. But the arrow points both ways.

> Sometimes companies enshittify because shareholders demand extraction.
> Sometimes they enshittify because customers teach them that trust is too
> expensive.

## A modest proposal

People used to argue for mandatory military service on the grounds that it built
discipline and a sense of shared obligation.

I would like to propose an update. One semester of customer support, retail, or
a fast food counter during the lunch rush. Not as punishment. Just long enough
to learn that the person on the other side of the counter is also a person, and
that they can tell when you are being unkind to them because of the name at the
bottom of the email.

Andrew would like that very much.
